1. The idea

Books on menswear mostly teach by example: this man in this jacket with this tie. That helps only as far as you resemble the man in the picture. Underneath the examples sit a few rules that are well understood and can be measured. Valet measures you, applies the rules and tells you why each verdict holds, so you can apply the reasoning yourself.

Every rule carries an evidence badge:

  • A Perception science. Measured and replicated: colour difference, simultaneous contrast, the visibility of fine detail.
  • B Convention. Long-standing, widely agreed practice in tailoring and colour theory. Stable across sources, but not experiments.
  • C Popular system. Widely used but not validated, such as "seasonal" colour analysis. Valet does not use these to score you.

Most rules have two halves: a quantity that can be measured (A) and a judgement about what looks good at a given value (B). Where a rule shows both badges, the measurement is science and the threshold is convention. Every number that is a threshold or a weight is a starting value, written down so it can be argued with.

How Valet uses its rules. Every check follows the same path. First your colouring is measured (sections 2 and 3), and it does not change while you dress. Then each piece is reduced to what is seen of it across a room: its colour as it adds up from 3 m, its lightness, its pattern and how visible that is, its cloth and its type. Each rule then looks at one relationship (a colour against your skin, the shirt against the layer over it, two patterns side by side) and returns a score, the reason in words, its evidence badge and its kind. The verdict counts what fails and what to note; suggestions and Choose the best colours try other colours and re-check the whole outfit before saying anything. Each rule is written down once, with its name, weight, kind and the section here that explains it, and each threshold is named once in the code with its badge, so a number can be found, argued with and changed in one place. Tests run every rule against outfits where it should and should not apply, and thousands of random outfits, before any change is published.

Where the conventions come from. Most B rules were first written in two unpublished working documents, the Style Guide and the Outfit Check card (section 19), and are being traced to the published menswear literature. Where a published source states the same convention, it is named beside the rule; where none has been found yet, the rule says it rests on the Style Guide; and where the Style Guide made a choice of its own (two patterns at the face rather than three, for a bald head), the text says so.

Four kinds of rule. Besides its badge, every outfit rule is one of four kinds, and the kind decides how it counts. Perceptual rules describe what the eye measurably does (badge A or A/B): the colour at your face, the light-dark step, a near match, patterns at one scale, a pattern louder than the face, a tonal column. Conventions are the tailoring and colour conventions (badge B): formality, season, one metal and the rest. Both are judged, and the badge says which is which. Possibility rules say what cannot be worn at all: a pattern its cloth does not carry, a pocket square with no breast pocket, a buttonhole flower with no lapel, neckwear with no collar, cufflinks with no cuffs. The Dresser does not let you put these together; if one arrives anyway (from a file), the verdict says the outfit is not possible as set, rather than judging how it looks. Context is advice about what does not change with the outfit: your frames against your face, the collar for your face, trousers for your build. It is shown as information and never counted in a verdict. The thresholds are written in one place in the code, each with its badge and the section here that explains it.

How a convention could become science. A B threshold can be tested the way perception scientists test illusions. Show the same photograph twice, recoloured at the collar, ask which looks better, and repeat it many times in random order. Where the choices split evenly is the threshold for that face and those observers: the method of paired comparisons (Thurstone 1927), close to the one Thompson and Mikellidou used for stripes (section 11). Nobody has yet run this on Valet's thresholds, so they stay B. Two limits would remain: a recoloured photo is not cloth (no folds, texture or light thrown back from it), and one person's choices measure that person's taste, not how others see him.

When a popular rule fails the evidence. Some rules everyone repeats are contradicted by experiments, or have never been tested. Valet does not quietly drop them: section 16 lists them, with what the studies found and what Valet does instead.

What Valet cannot do. It is a compass, not a mirror or a tailor:

  • It reads only what it is given. A colour reading from a photo is as good as the light it was taken in; section 2 lists what goes wrong. If the photo is doubtful, choose the features by name, and let the daylight tests on your profile settle what remains.
  • It measures; it does not feel or see. Most bodies are a little asymmetric (one shoulder lower, one arm longer), posture varies beyond the three shape settings, two cloths cut to the same measurements hang differently, and every house cuts its sizes its own way. That is why every target is a range and every size a starting point (sections 13 to 15): a garment that fits you in front of a mirror beats any number here.
  • It knows everyday dress. The occasions run from casual to evening. The fixed codes of black tie, white tie and morning dress, and the finer points of period accessories, are not checked; a guide to those codes is the better source.
  • It is one input. Colour and proportion are part of dressing well; fit, occasion and the way clothes are worn matter as much, as the tailoring literature in section 18 keeps saying.
  • It asks for some effort. Your skin is enough to start. The measurements are optional; six can be taken alone, six are easier with help, and three (shoulder width, arm and back-neck to floor) need a second person.

Rules can be broken on purpose. A break reads as intentional when it is repeated and anchored by a calm base; the Dresser allows one such break, a loud repeat or a strong colour below the belt, and names it as the outfit's bold move rather than a mistake. Whether a break is repeated elsewhere is not checked.

2. Measuring colour from a photo

A camera records colour under whatever light falls on you. Warm indoor light makes everyone look golden; a cloudy window makes everyone look grey. So Valet asks you to sample a sheet of white paper in the same photo.

A The correction is a von Kries adaptation: the sample is converted to cone-like signals (the Bradford transform used in colour management), each signal is scaled so that the paper becomes a neutral white at CIELAB L* ≈ 95, and the result is converted back. The same scaling is applied to your skin, hair and eye samples, which also normalises exposure. Each sample is averaged over a small patch, in linear light, to suppress noise.

Colours are then converted from sRGB to CIELAB (CIE 1976, D65 white): L* is lightness (0 black to 100 white), a* runs green to red, b* blue to yellow. Chroma C* is the distance from grey, and the hue angle h is the direction: the measured form of Munsell's hue, value and chroma (1905).

A colour you set by hand, with the tone ladder and sliders, is taken as the true colour and is not corrected.

Limits.
  • Phone cameras process images: HDR, beauty filters, "warmth", and a tone curve that makes "linear light" only approximately linear. Daylight, no filter and paper close to the face give the best result.
  • A Metamerism. Two cloths dyed differently can match under one light and part under another, because they reach the same colour with different mixes of wavelengths (CIE 015:2018). A camera, like the eye, sees only the result under the light of the moment. So check a jacket against the trousers you mean to wear with it by a window, not only under the shop's lamps, and trust daylight over Valet's screen.
  • Office paper contains optical brighteners that glow slightly blue in daylight. Treating that paper as neutral nudges every reading a little warm. This is one reason the temperature reading is never given more than medium confidence unless it is far from neutral.
  • Paper that is blown out to pure white, or in shadow while the face is lit, cannot be used: Valet warns, and leaves the colours uncorrected rather than correct them by a wrong white. Strongly coloured paper is still corrected, which is what the Bradford transform is for, with a warning to check the sample.
  • One white sample corrects one light. Under a lamp and a window at once, the paper and the face can be lit by different mixes of the two, and no single correction is right for both. Take the photo by a window with the lamps off.
  • Shine and shadow on the skin itself are not corrected. A sample in shadow reads deeper than the skin is, one on a shiny patch lighter and greyer. Sample the cheek or forehead where the light is even and the skin is matt.
  • When the photo cannot be trusted, choose the features by name instead (section 3), and settle doubts with the daylight tests on your profile.
Treat the result as a good estimate, not a laboratory measurement.

3. The four dimensions of your colouring

Contrast A B
The strongest lightness step (ΔL*) between your skin and a feature: hair, eyes, and the whites of the eyes. Below 25 is low, 25–45 medium, above 45 high. Lightness contrast between features and skin is a studied property of faces (Russell 2009); using it to choose clothes is the tailoring convention (Flusser 2002). Three refinements, all working values:
  • Hair. Hair frames the whole face, so its step counts in full. With no hair, the same colour survives only in the brows, a thin line; that step counts at 80%.
  • Eyes. The iris is the smallest feature, so its step counts at 60%. At talking distance it spans well under a degree of visual angle, so its edge with the skin lies at finer spatial frequencies than the hairline's, where the eye's contrast sensitivity falls (Campbell and Robson 1968; section 11): the same lightness step counts for less (A for the mechanism, B for the 60%). Counted in full, a fair man with white hair and dark eyes read as high contrast, which is not what one sees.
  • Whites of the eyes. Not sampled; assumed at L* 80 and counted at 80%. On fair skin this adds nothing. On deep skin it is the strongest light-dark step in the face, and leaving it out would wrongly read deep skin with dark hair as low contrast.
Depth A
The average lightness of what is seen, weighted by area: skin 1, eyes 0.08, and hair 0.08 (brows only), 0.45 (short) or 0.85 (long). A fair, bald head is light; the same face under long dark hair is medium.
Temperature lean B
The hue angle of your skin in CIELAB. Skin hues sit roughly between 45° (pinker, cooler) and 70° (more golden, warmer); Valet maps 57° to neutral and ±8° to fully cool or warm. This is the least reliable of the four. The camera limits above act on exactly this number, and skin hue also moves with tanning and with depth, so a single neutral point for everyone is crude. It carries a confidence label, and verdicts that rest on a low-confidence reading say so.
Chroma A
How vivid your own colouring is: the plain mean C* of your features. Unweighted on purpose: the eyes are small, but they are where people look.

Names instead of numbers

B You can set each feature by name. A name stands for a typical colour, so the reading is coarser than a good photo but far better than a guess on three sliders, and it uses words you already know. The sliders remain under "Fine-tune the colour" for anyone who wants them.

  • Skin has two parts, as colorimetry measures it: how light or dark it is (L*), and the direction of its colour, its undertone (the hue angle, section 7). Valet offers ten depths from very fair to very deep and three undertones. Pink sets the skin's hue 7° below Valet's neutral point and golden 7° above, which reads as cool and warm; "neutral or not sure" sits on it. The home tests for undertone (wrist veins, white paper in daylight, whether you burn or tan) are in the "?" beside the control. They are rough at best (section 16 explains why the vein test fails), and the burn-or-tan question really measures something else: it is the basis of the dermatologists' Fitzpatrick phototypes (1975), which describe reaction to sun, not colour. Skin colour itself is classed in dermatology by the individual typology angle, computed from the same L* and b* Valet measures (Chardon and others 1991), and in imaging by the ten-tone Monk scale (2023).
  • Hair uses the hairdresser's chart, the one naming system for hair that people meet in daily life: ten depth levels from 1 (black) through the browns (2 to 5) to the blonds (6 dark blond, 7 blond, up to 10, the lightest), a tone (ash, natural, golden), and the natural reds (auburn, copper red, strawberry blond). Anthropology has its own charts, the Fischer–Saller scale for hair and the Martin–Schultz scale for eyes; the everyday words cover the same ground.
  • Eyes run from blue through grey, green, hazel and amber to dark brown.

When a colour comes from a photo or from the sliders, Valet shows the closest name beside it, so you can see where the measurement lands in ordinary words.

Winter and summer

A B Skin is the one part of your colouring that changes through the year. Sun makes it darker and a little redder. The best measurement Valet could find followed 103 students through two weeks of outdoor sun in late summer: on the forehead, cheek and neck, lightness fell by 2.5 to 2.9 L*, red rose by up to about 1 a*, yellow hardly moved, and the hue angle shifted by under 2.5° toward red (Jiang et al. 2022). The students were young adults in north-east China, outdoors for military training, so the size of a tan on other skins and in other sun may differ; the direction is the robust part. That is the A part: the direction and size of a light tan.

So Valet keeps two skins. Winter is your base profile. Summer is one of three:

  • An estimate, the default: your winter skin with the measured two-week change applied. It is a light tan. If you tan more, it underestimates.
  • Same as winter, for people who hardly tan.
  • Your own: the depth you reach by late summer, picked by name, or skin sampled from a summer photo with its own sheet of white paper. Hair and eyes keep the correction from your first photo.

Keep your winter undertone when you pick a summer depth. The hue barely moves with a tan, so pink skin stays pink, only darker. Choosing "golden" for summer because the skin looks browner would wrongly turn your palette warm.

Every rule then runs on the summer skin, and the effects follow from the arithmetic rather than from separate summer rules (B):

  • Pale colours separate better. White, ivory and écru sat close to fair winter skin; against a tan they stand apart, so they often move up a tier.
  • Contrast to dark hair falls; to light hair it rises. Darker skin closes the gap to dark hair and opens it to blond. (Sun also lightens hair, which Valet does not model.)
  • Colours near the tan itself lose ground. Camel, sand and some pinks can come too close to tanned skin.

There is one experiment on exactly this question. Perrett and Sprengelmeyer (2021) found observers chose cooler blues to go with fair skin and warmer orange-reds with tanned skin. Valet's tan estimate does not make skin warmer by hue, so that preference reaches the scores only through depth and separation. The temperature rule stays at B.

The profile card shows both seasons side by side and lists every colour whose tier moves. The switch at the top of the page decides which season every tab shows.

What the seasons change besides skin, and Valet does not model. The light and the surroundings change too. In winter, at least in northern Europe, the sun is low and weak, the sky is often overcast and streets, buildings and bare trees are grey; in summer the sun is high and strong and the surroundings are green and bright.

  • A Brighter light makes colours look more colourful and contrasts look stronger (the Hunt and Stevens effects, both part of the CIE's colour-appearance models, CIECAM02 and its 2022 successor CIECAM16). Under a grey winter sky the same cloth looks quieter and darker; in summer sun it looks louder, and strong contrasts look harder.
  • B Surroundings set what stands out. Against grey winter streets a coloured piece is the brightest thing around; against summer green and strong light, pale and light colours look fresh and very dark ones heavy.
  • Why the tiers do not move for this. Your face and your clothes are lit by the same light and seen against the same surroundings, so how a colour sits against your skin, which is what the tiers measure, changes far less than how the colour looks on its own. What changes is the overall volume: a vivid colour that is right in winter can be loud in July, and a pale outfit that is fresh in July can look washed out in February. That is the reason for the daylight test: judge cloth in the light, and the season, you will wear it in.

Grey hair

A B Greying hair is pigmented strands and white strands side by side. A hair is under a tenth of a millimetre wide, far finer than the eye resolves at talking distance, so the head reads as one blended colour: the same fact that turns a fine stripe into a solid (section 11). That much is perception science. How light the blend is, is less certain. Flat patches would average in linear light, but hair is a translucent mass in which dark strands shadow white ones, and plain averaging makes a quarter-grey head far lighter than it looks. Valet blends your pigmented colour with white hair in CIELAB, which falls between the two. It is a working model.

You give the colour your hair has where it still has pigment, and the share of grey. The blend then feeds every reading, and three things follow:

  • Contrast moves, and the direction depends on your skin. On lighter skin grey closes the gap between hair and skin, so contrast falls: dark brown hair on fair skin reads high, at half grey medium, and black beside a white shirt stops being a safe choice. On deep skin grey opens the gap, so contrast rises. The direction falls out of measuring the blended hair; there is no separate rule for it.
  • Blond hardly changes. Blond and grey are about equally light, so the step to the skin barely moves. The same guides put blond and greying hair in one group for this reason.
  • Colouring becomes more muted. Grey has almost no chroma, so your own chroma falls and very vivid colours overpower you sooner.

Limits. Grey rarely arrives evenly; temples and beard usually go first, and Valet uses one figure for the whole head. Up close, salt-and-pepper hair is itself a fine pattern, which the score ignores. Separately from hair, the contrast between features and skin falls with age and is one of the cues by which age is judged (Porcheron, Mauger and Russell 2013, measured on women's faces), so a reading taken ten years ago is worth repeating.

4. Contrast matching

A B The eye reads a face as balanced when the contrast of what surrounds it is in the same range as the face's own contrast. If the clothes near the face are much more contrasty than the face, they take the attention; if they are much less, the face loses definition. Valet applies this in two places.

A colour against your skin. Each colour is compared with a band around your face contrast:

lower = max(8, 0.5 × contrast)    upper = 1.5 × contrast + 15

The lower bound is about definition, which can come from lightness or from hue, so it is tested against the larger of ΔL* and 1.2 × ΔE2000. The upper bound is about heaviness, which is lightness alone, so it is tested against ΔL*. Inside the band scores fully; outside, the score falls off over 20 units. The profile card draws this band in green on your lightness scale.

Shirt against the layer over it. The lightness chart and the Dresser check that the shirt and the layer that meets it at the neck differ by at least the lower bound, so the face has a frame. It only objects to a very strong step when your own contrast is low.

A B Glasses. A frame is the one line that is always at the face, so the same band judges it. The lightness step between an acetate frame, in the colour you choose, and your skin is compared with the upper bound: above it, the frame is the strongest edge at the face and is seen before the face. On a low-contrast face a black frame usually crosses it, and honey tortoiseshell, grey, clear or thin metal frames sit with the face instead; on a high-contrast face the black frame belongs. Metal frames are thin lines and clear frames show the skin through them, so only acetate is judged, and its step is measured from the colour you set. Glasses are worn with an outfit, so they are set among the accessories in the Dresser and saved with each outfit, and the verdict appears there.

Limit. The convention was written from mostly light-skinned examples. The whites-of-the-eyes term is Valet's own way of correcting that: on deep skin the step between the skin and the whites of the eyes is the strongest in the face. The whites of the eyes are a small feature, like the iris, so their step counts at the same reduced weight (see the iris above); a very deep face with black hair and dark eyes then reads as medium contrast rather than high, and lighter hair or grey raises it.

5. Separation from the skin

A B When a colour is close to your skin in lightness and hue, face and garment separate weakly. Valet measures the distance with CIEDE2000, the standard formula for perceived colour difference, and scores it from 0 at ΔE 10 to full at ΔE 20.

Those two numbers are convention, not science. A ΔE of 10 between two flat patches is plainly visible; the claim is only that, across a soft boundary with shadow and texture and at talking distance, differences in this range give the face a weak edge. CIEDE2000 was also built for small differences and is less exact for large ones. Beige, camel and oatmeal usually fail here for fair skin.

So does white, a little: on fair skin it sits about as far from the face as écru does, and lands in tier 2. Its usual job is to be the light half of a light-dark step under a dark jacket, which section 4 scores separately.

6. Simultaneous contrast (Chevreul)

A B In 1839 the chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul, working on dyes at the Gobelins tapestry works, showed that two colours placed side by side each push the other toward its opposite. A green collar makes the skin above it look redder; a yellow one makes fair skin look bluer, which reads as grey or tired; blue and violet push skin slightly warmer.

The direction of the push is science. Its size on a real face is not well measured, and whether a given push flatters is a judgement. Valet estimates it by moving your skin's a* and b* one tenth of the garment's own a* and b* in the opposite direction, then checks whether the result emphasises something unwanted: ruddiness, a grey cast or sallowness.

A second, physical effect works the other way: light reflected off the garment tints the jaw and neck with the garment's own colour. Valet does not model it.

A B Redness (set on the You tab). A red or pink beside a ruddy skin is echoed by the red in it and picks it out; green, the complement of red, makes the skin look redder by simultaneous contrast. Colour analysts give the same rule: to de-emphasise a colour in your colouring, avoid its complement, so sallow skin looks more yellow beside purple (NMSU Extension, Guide C-315). Both effects grow with saturation and shrink for dark, muted colours; blues and greys are the calm choice.

7. Temperature and chroma

B Colours are warm (reds, oranges, yellows, olive, camel) or cool (blues, blue-greens, violets), with blue-reds such as burgundy and rose near neutral. Valet derives this from the hue angle and weakens it for greyish colours. Near the face, a colour's temperature should agree with your lean; if your lean is neutral, only strong extremes are penalised.

There is one direct experiment. Perrett and Sprengelmeyer (2021) had observers choose clothing colours for photographed faces and found cooler blues preferred for fair skin and warmer orange-reds for tanned skin. That supports matching temperature to skin, but it compared fair with tanned skin in white women, not pink with golden undertone, so the rule stays at B.

A B One angle, two pigments. Your lean is a single number, the hue angle of the skin: the direction between its redness (a*) and its yellowness (b*). Skin research keeps the two apart. Yellowness comes mainly from melanin and from carotenoids in the diet, and observers judge yellower, lighter faces as healthier (Stephen, Coetzee and Perrett 2011); redness comes mostly from blood in the skin. One angle hides which of them moved, so a ruddy face and a sallow one can share a hue. Your profile shows a* and b* beside the angle, and redness has a rule of its own (the Redness setting, section 6). Thresholds that would use a* and b* separately in the tiers have not been tested, so the angle remains the one temperature input.

C Not used: a "healthy glow" bonus. By simultaneous contrast a blue or violet collar pushes the skin beside it a little towards yellow, and yellower skin looks healthier in the study above. Putting the two together would reward blues near the face. Neither study tested the combination, and looking healthy is not the same as being well dressed, so Valet gives no such bonus.

B A colour much more saturated than your own colouring (more than about 35 C* above it) arrives before you do. The penalty grows with the excess.

B Metal frames are a second temperature at the face. The Dresser judges them with the outfit they are worn with: gold frames beside a warm colour are noted unless your own colouring is warm, where the two agree; silver frames beside a cool colour only if your colouring is warm. An acetate frame counts the same way by its own colour, warm or cool by its hue and scaled down for low chroma as any colour is: tortoiseshell is warm, navy cool, black and grey neutral. The palette is your colours on their own, without frames.

8. From scores to tiers

The rules combine into one score at the face. The weights are a judgement about relative importance, not a measurement:

0.30 contrast + 0.20 separation + 0.25 temperature + 0.25 chroma − Chevreul and frame penalties

A colour is also only as good as its worst failing, so each tier has a floor for the weakest rule:

  • Tier 1 — anywhere: score ≥ 0.80 and no rule below 0.70. The base of a wardrobe.
  • Tier 2 — deliberate colour: score ≥ 0.62 and no rule below 0.45. Fine at the face as the one chosen colour of an outfit.
  • Tier 3 — below the belt: fails the above, but is neither more vivid than your colouring nor heavier than your face carries (camel or rust on a cool face, a pale colour that blends with the skin). Trousers, shoes and belts.
  • Tier 4 — never at the face: fails, and the chroma rule says it is more vivid than your colouring, or the contrast rule says it is heavier than your face carries: "too vivid or too much". Small accents, or one bold piece below the belt.

The colour map on the Palette tab runs the same score over every hue and lightness at one strength. Hue and lightness there are OKLCH, so neighbouring squares are even steps to the eye, and squares your screen cannot show are left empty. The size of a square is its verdict, so the shape can be read without judging colour: large for tier 1, large with a white dot for tier 2, small for tiers 3 and 4. The Strength slider sets the saturation of the whole map; moving it shows how far toward vivid your palette reaches.

9. Pairing

B Pairings use OKLCH (Ottosson, 2020), a colour space in which equal steps look roughly equal, so hue relationships are more trustworthy than on the old artist's wheel.

  • Neutrals (OKLCH chroma below 0.04) pair with anything, provided the lightness differs clearly. When the base itself is a neutral, any hue is free.
  • Analogous colours lie within about 35° of hue: calm and safe.
  • Complementary colours lie about 180° apart (±30°): lively, best when one of them is dark or muted. These are perceptual opposites, which differ from the painter's wheel: the opposite of blue is a yellow-orange, and the opposite of red is a blue-green rather than green.
  • Any pair needs a lightness difference of at least about 12 L*, or the two read as one blur.

Navy, camel and tan are neutrals in the wardrobe sense but not by this measure; they carry enough colour to have a hue, and are paired as colours.

A useful outfit template: two neutrals and one colour (tonal dressing in neutrals with just one colour added: Permanent Style, 2024).

Build around one colour

B Tailors and shops once chose a colour for the season and built everything around it. On the Palette tab you can do the same for yourself: choose one colour, and Valet says where it can go on you (its tier), and why when it is weak. For a colour that does not suit you near the face it offers its nearest versions that do (mustard leads to sage and moss), and it refuses the face outright for a tier 4 colour, allowing at most a small accent far from it. Around the colour it lists neutrals that suit you and separate clearly from it, colours that relate to it as neighbours or opposites and suit you, and colours to keep apart from it: near matches and colours that compete. "Put it in the study" puts the colour where it belongs, with a neutral and, where it helps, an accent chosen to go with it.

10. The outfit as a whole

B Once there is a whole outfit, rules apply between pieces. These are the Outfit Check card's questions, made measurable. The card is the one-page checklist that goes with the Style Guide (both unpublished; section 19): six questions (temperature, contrast, frame, sheen, pattern, metal), then the feet; the question numbers below are its numbers. All are convention unless badged otherwise.

  • Positions, not fixed garments. An outfit is a set of positions: a hat, next to the neck, neckwear, a mid layer, an outer layer, a scarf, a pocket square, trousers, socks, shoes and belt. Only next to the neck, trousers and shoes are needed, so a summer shirt and trousers is a whole outfit. Each position holds a garment type: a shirt, roll neck, polo or T-shirt; a tie, bow tie or cravat (ascot); a crew neck, V-neck, cardigan or waistcoat; a jacket, overshirt or coat. A waistcoat, like a V-neck, wants a shirt collar under it; with the lab link, Proportion checks that its points meet the trousers' waistband. Neckwear sits at the collar and needs one; the scarf is its own position, worn over everything, so a tie and a scarf can go together under a coat. The type carries what the rules need: whether it frames the face, which patterns are used on it, and the largest pattern scale that suits it (section 11).
  • At the face. Every layer above the waist, and the pocket square, is scored at the face, using the colour it adds up to from a distance (section 11 for patterns). Each should be tier 1 or 2.
  • Something frames the face. The Outfit Check card's third question: name the thing that frames the face. Flusser puts it as "think of the face as the picture and what surrounds it as the frame" (Dressing the Man), and a white collar separating head and neck is a long-standing observation (Permanent Style, 2024). A shirt or polo collar, a roll neck, a shawl collar, lapels, a coat collar or a scarf all count. A T-shirt or crew neck alone does not, which is noted as casual rather than failed. A tie needs a shirt to sit under: a collar and a full button front, so a polo, whose placket stops halfway, does not take one. A V-neck knit is cut to show a collar; a waistcoat wants a shirt that takes a tie.
  • Light-dark step A B. The shirt and the layer that meets it at the neck should differ by at least the lower bound of section 4. That layer is, in the order they sit there: a scarf, which lies against the collar whatever is worn over the shirt (a scarf is narrow, so when the knit or jacket beside it over the shirt front makes the stronger step, that one counts); else a knit or waistcoat, which goes on before the jacket; else the jacket; else a coat worn straight over the shirt; else a tie. The bound is the one a colour has to clear against your skin. A hat, or a coat over a jacket, cannot stand in for the step: the frame is the line where the shirt meets what is over it. With a single layer, the collar alone frames the face, which is noted but not failed.
  • One temperature above the waist. A warm piece among cool ones, or the reverse, reads as the odd one out.
  • Colours that compete. Wardrobe neutrals (OKLCH chroma under 0.06) are free. Coloured pieces are grouped into families within 35° of hue. One family is the classic template. Two families are fine when they are complementary (best if one is dark or muted) or when one is only a small accent (tie, shoes, a pattern's second colour). Two families that are neither neighbours nor opposites compete. Three or more is flagged.
  • Near match A B. Two pieces between ΔE2000 1.5 and 7 apart, such as navy with midnight, are close enough to look intended to match and far enough to fail. Below 1.5 they match: a jacket and trousers that match read as a suit.
  • Top and bottom differ by at least 12 L*, or the halves blur.
  • One statement. A tier 2 colour is "the one chosen colour of an outfit" (section 8). A second, different tier 2 colour at the face competes with it, and so does a tier 4 colour below the belt, which is the bold move of a bold day. Two statements are flagged as a caution, three as a failure. Neutrals do not count, and two pieces in one colour count once.
  • Tie against shirt. The tie should separate from the shirt; darker than the shirt is the reliable order.
  • One colour, one piece. A colour works as one piece among neutrals (rule 4). The same colour, or one colour family, over the top and the trousers reads as a uniform. Wardrobe neutrals here are the menswear ones (navy, greys, black, white, creams and browns; a colour within ΔE2000 6 of a library colour counts as that colour, so one sampled from a photo that has drifted a little keeps its name), so tonal charcoal or all-black evening dress passes, but a forest shirt over forest trousers does not, even though forest is as muted as navy. A coloured suit, jacket and trousers cut as one, is the exception.
  • The card's order. The checks are grouped as the Outfit Check card asks them: 1 temperature, 2 contrast, 3 frame, 4 sheen, 5 pattern, 6 metal and accessories, then the feet; after them come the colours together, formality and season. A row of the groups at the top shows each as passed, to note or failed; click one to go to it.
  • Cloth. Every piece has a cloth as well as a colour: worsted, flannel, hopsack, tweed, linen, fresco, cotton twill, corduroy, denim for tailoring; poplin, oxford, twill, linen, brushed cotton for shirts; merino, lambswool, cashmere for knits; grenadine, silk twill, knitted silk, wool for ties; calf and suede for shoes; and so on, as the Style Guide lists them. Each cloth carries how formal it is, its season, and whether it shines. A piece is as formal as the lower of its type and its cloth: a tweed jacket is country wear, not business.
  • 4. Sheen (Outfit Check, question 4). That too much shine reads as cheap cloth is common tailoring advice (Black Lapel on polyester; Permanent Style on very fine worsteds being shinier); Valet applies it most where it is seen most: anything shiny near the face (satin ties, lustrous worsted, glossy nylon) is noted as reading cheap, and below the belt it is a caution; patent shoes belong to black tie, and black-tie patent is black.
  • Formality and the occasion. Choose the occasion at the top: casual, smart casual, business or evening. A dress shirt worn without a tie is open-collared, smart rather than business, so it counts a step lower; dark denim sits at relaxed, the register of jeans under a blazer, not with sportswear. Evening means dinner or an event, not a dress code: black tie, white tie and morning dress have fixed codes of their own, which Valet does not check. Pieces are placed on a five-step scale from casual to business, and each occasion has its range. Pieces four steps apart look like a mistake; three apart is a deliberate mix at best. The Style Guide's own pairings are checked by name (that trainers with a suit look disharmonious in the classic view, while mixing formalities is a current trend, is Gentleman's Gazette's account too): chunky trainers fail with anything smart; a tie with trainers fails; a suit with trainers is a fashion look rather than a classic one; a tie with denim needs a rough tie and jacket (Permanent Style: with jeans, rougher and more casual ties, knitted wool or silk; Gentleman's Gazette goes further and leaves the tie off with jeans altogether). A jacket and trousers in one colour but two cloths look like a suit jacket worn with the wrong trousers.
  • Season. Cloth against the season switch: flannel, tweed and heavy knits belong to winter, linen, fresco, seersucker and piqué to summer, and summer and winter cloth in one outfit look as if one of them was a mistake.
  • Below the belt. Tier 4 is allowed as the one bold move. Shoes are the trousers' depth or darker; light warm shoes clash with a cool top.
  • Socks match the trousers or go darker, so the leg line runs unbroken into the shoe; a deep colour (burgundy, forest) is the one small play allowed there. No white socks with shoes (Style Guide, section 5). Matching the socks to the trousers rather than the shoes lengthens the leg line, and a tone or two darker, or a harmonising deep colour (forest green with grey, purple with navy), is the classic latitude (Permanent Style, 2026; also Garmany).
  • Pocket square. It needs a jacket with a breast pocket. White linen is never wrong; a square in the tie's exact cloth looks bought as a set.

Accessories

B Metal (Outfit Check, question 6): one metal through the outfit, a two-tone watch the one exception. This is the most widely repeated accessory rule (Gentleman's Gazette, Art of Manliness, Real Men Real Style), and the same writers allow a ring or an heirloom to stand outside it. Metal blazer buttons and a lapel pin count, even with no watch or belt on, and a clash offers to change the buttons (to the other metal, or to horn) as well as the watch or buckle. A warm metal against cool colouring is a stray warm note. A ring worn every day is let stand outside the matching rule. Glasses and an earring are at the face, so they are compared with each other rather than with the belt. Stones are small accents: they look chosen when they echo a colour worn (lapis with navy, garnet with a burgundy tie).

B Size, each with its reason:

  • Watch: 36 to 40 mm by default, or a range from your wrist (from 34–38 mm under 15.5 cm up to 40–44 mm over 18.5 cm). A larger case overhangs the wrist and will not go under a shirt cuff.
  • Cufflinks: about 12 to 18 mm, showing a little below the jacket sleeve. Only with a shirt, since a polo or T-shirt has no cuff to take them. Valet does not record whether a shirt has double (French) cuffs, so it does not guess them from the cloth; check yours.
  • Tie bar: about three quarters of the tie's width, so up to 60 mm on an 8 cm tie. Longer than the tie is wide, its ends stick out.
  • Ring: a signet or band face up to about 15 mm.
  • Earring: a stud up to about 5 mm. The pupil is 2 to 8 mm and the iris about 12 mm; a stud of pupil size reads as a point of light beside the face and the eyes stay the focus. Larger, it competes with them.

Over the range, the check lists what elsewhere in the outfit carries the piece, for example a large stud with a matte or dark face, metal matching the frames and no pattern at the face, and each one in place lifts it. Far over the range (a stud past 8 mm, a tie bar wider than the tie), nothing compensates and the check says to take it off for this outfit.

B Coats, jackets and gloves. A coat is its own layer over the jacket or knit: overcoat, Chesterfield, covert coat, trench coat, peacoat, duffle coat or parka. It frames the face like a jacket, but its formality is the register of the street and it comes off indoors, so it is given more room than other pieces. Two things are noted: a coat far from the occasion (a parka or duffle coat over business or evening dress), and a coat five steps below what it covers, such as a parka over a worsted suit, whatever the occasion. Five is one step more than the four that mark a mistake between other pieces (Formality, above). Jackets include the blazer, whose metal buttons count as metal in the outfit and so join the one-metal rule; the default buttons are silver, so a new blazer does not start with a clash. The casual waxed, field and Harrington jackets frame the face with a collar rather than lapels. Gloves are cloth like any other piece: glove leather and knitted wool are winter cloth and join the season rule (suede is worn all year), knitted gloves are casual and noted with business or evening dress, and in the colour count they are a small piece. A hat is judged by its type against the occasion, with a step of grace either side: a homburg or bowler with casual dress looks borrowed, a baseball cap or beanie with business or evening dress too casual. Each of these notes offers a fix, and no fix touches a piece you have locked.

B The buttonhole. Three things go through it, and they differ in what there is to set. A fresh flower has only its colour, since it has no cloth. A fabric flower, of silk or linen, keeps; it has a cloth as well as a colour, and the cloth brings the sheen and season rules with it, as on a tie (a linen flower is summer cloth). A lapel pin has only its metal. All three go in the buttonhole of the left lapel, so it needs a jacket, blazer or coat with lapels; a casual jacket or a knit has none. That is a physical requirement, and the only buttonhole rule. Near the face its colour is scored like any other piece there, and in the colour count it is a small piece, like the tie or the pocket square. A lapel pin is worn metal and joins the one-metal rule.

B Shoes. A shoe's material is its upper: calf, suede or patent. Spectators are two-tone: the toe cap, heel and lace panel are in a second colour. The rules read them as their two colours, never as a pattern, and the second colour counts in the colour count as a small piece. Their formality comes from their type and cloth like any other shoe's.

B The watch beyond its size. A digital display or a rubber strap reads as sport, so both are noted with business or evening dress. That is the formality register applied to the watch, and nothing more is judged. Whether a watch is mechanical or quartz cannot be seen, so it is not judged either.

Each had a number or a list with no derivation behind it. They may be good advice, but Valet only checks what it can explain, so they are left to your judgement.

Keep this, fix the rest

For a piece you own that does not fit the outfit. If the kept piece still fails afterwards, no change elsewhere can mend it. When that failure is its colour at the face, it is not for you near the face, though the colour can still be worn lower down; otherwise it is not for this outfit.

What still works here

B A tier says whether a colour suits you. The next choice also depends on what you already have on, and on where the new piece sits: navy is tier 1 for most men and still wrong as trousers under a navy jacket of a slightly different shade. So the library in the Outfit editor marks each colour for the piece you are choosing, and the palette on the Palette tab marks each colour for the selected place of the study (near the face, an accent, below the belt).   • Struck through: it would make a rule fail. At the face that includes every tier 3 and 4 colour; below the belt it does not.
  • "!": it would bend a rule, for example a second deliberate colour.
  • Unmarked: it keeps every rule as well as any colour could.

A colour is blamed only for what it causes. If two other pieces already clash, no choice for this garment can mend that, so nothing is struck out on that account and the problem is named separately. The marks change when you select another piece or place, because the rules differ by place: a shirt has to make a light-dark step with the jacket, a tie has to stand clear of the shirt, trousers have to separate from the top, shoes should be no lighter than the trousers. 11. Patterns

A pattern is not one thing at every distance. Up close a birdseye is a field of dots; across a room it is plain navy. Valet works out what is left of a cloth at 1 m (talking), 3 m (across a room) and 10 m (across a street, so in overcast daylight of about 5,000 lux rather than room light), and the Outfit editor shows it under "From a distance".

How big a pattern looks

A What reaches the eye is not the size of a pattern but the angle it fills: a repeat of p centimetres seen from d metres spans 2·atan(p / 2d). One centimetre fills 34 minutes of arc at 1 m, 11 at 3 m and 3.4 at 10 m. Vision science counts detail in cycles per degree: how many light-dark repeats fit into one degree. The same 1 cm check is 1.7 c/deg at 1 m and 17 at 10 m.

What the eye can see

A How much contrast the eye needs to see a fine stripe depends on its size. Campbell and Robson (1968) measured this as the contrast sensitivity function. Sensitivity peaks at a few cycles per degree and falls steeply for finer detail, reaching the limit of sight at around 40 to 50 c/deg in good light. Valet uses Barten's model of it (1999) with the parameters of ITU-R report BT.2246, checked against the colour-science library's implementation. Sensitivity also falls in dim light, and dark cloth sends less light: in a 300 lux room the limit is about 49 c/deg for white cloth and 39 for very dark cloth.

A Every cloth is a sum of stripes. Any repeating pattern can be split into simple sine-wave gratings (its Fourier components), and the eye judges each roughly on its own. Valet measured the components of each of its fourteen drawings. A component is visible when its contrast is above the eye's threshold at its size. A thin line or a small dot is made of many weak components that all peak at the line, so Valet adds the visible ones together; judging them one at a time would call a sharp windowpane line faint.

A Above threshold, contrast looks like contrast. Once a stripe is clearly visible, its apparent contrast hardly depends on its size (Georgeson and Sullivan 1975, "contrast constancy"). So Valet takes the strength of what is visible to be its contrast less the threshold.

A B Colour detail goes first. Red-green and blue-yellow differences without a light-dark difference reach their limit at about 11 to 12 c/deg (Mullen 1985), roughly a quarter of the limit for light and dark. A burgundy dot on navy keeps its colour only when it is large enough. Valet models this with a smooth factor that halves colour differences at 5 c/deg and removes nearly all of them by 12; the shape of that curve is an approximation.

Plain, texture or pattern

B The science says what is visible. Calling it texture or pattern is a convention, and these are Valet's working values:

  • Plain: nothing is visible.
  • Texture: something is visible, but only finer than 10 c/deg, so each light or dark element is under about 3 minutes of arc, three times the finest detail a sharp eye resolves (grain, not shapes). Or it is visible only faintly: under 5% contrast and under about 5 ΔE of colour.
  • Pattern: the rest.

A colour difference counts as visible from about ΔE 2.3, roughly the smallest difference most people notice between flat colours.

Across a room decides. Whether a cloth counts towards the pattern limits at the face is judged at 3 m, the distance the Style Guide says matters. It is also the far phase of what the anthropologist Edward Hall called social distance, 2.1 to 3.7 m, where formal business is done (Hall 1966; observations of American culture, so B). That turns several old rules of thumb into one measurement:

  • A navy birdseye or nailhead reads as plain or texture from 3 m, so it is a foundation, not a pattern. Even with a white dot it is only texture there: the dots are too small to read as shapes.
  • A tonal herringbone fades to texture between 1 m and 3 m; a contrasty one does not.
  • A 6 mm gingham is still a pattern across a room, in navy and white or in white and pale blue. Halve it to 3 mm and both read as texture there.
  • A 7 mm pin dot sits at the edge: its dots are about 5 mm apart, right at the 10 c/deg line from 3 m. A wider-spaced dot reads as pattern. The Style Guide counts pin-dot ties as a pattern; Valet's measurement says they are borderline.

A Tonal patterns, grey on grey or navy on navy, are judged by the same measurement as any other: their light-dark and colour contrast is low, so they turn into texture sooner, and a fine one reads as plain. A tonal pattern the eye can still read across a room counts as a pattern. Until version 1.8 a separate rule counted any pattern whose two colours were within ΔE2000 12 as texture; no menswear source states it, and the measurement already covers it, so it was removed.

Limits. The model describes an attentive viewer looking straight at flat cloth in a lit room (300 lux, a 40 cm field). In life people glance, cloth folds, and texture in the weave masks fine pattern, so the model tends to call visible things that would go unnoticed. Read "pattern" near a boundary as "noticeable if you look". The fourteen drawings are simplified, and real yarns are never two flat colours.

The rules for combining patterns

A From a distance a pattern becomes its average colour. For every colour rule a patterned cloth is scored as its two colours mixed by area, in linear light. A navy cloth with a fine white stripe reads lighter than navy.

B Scale is one repeat, in centimetres, checked by hand:

  • Under 1 cm: semi-solid.
  • 1 to 3 cm: small, about one or two finger widths. Shirts and ties.
  • 3 to 9 cm: medium, up to a palm. Jackets; and the only medium pattern in an outfit.
  • 9 to 15 cm: large. Coats.
  • Over 15 cm: too large to wear.

B Which pattern on which garment. Valet offers only the cloths the Style Guide uses on each garment type, and flags a scale above what suits it (one class above is a bold move, two is wrong):

  • Headwear: a flat cap in herringbone, houndstooth or glen check; beanies, fedoras and panamas plain. A brimmed hat also has a band and a brim: the band sits at the face, so its colour is judged there (a navy band helps a natural straw panama); the brim should follow the frame, narrow looking small on a tall or broad man and wide swamping a slight one. A hat sits against the face (on a bald head, against the scalp), so it is scored at the face like a collar and should be tier 1 or 2. It adds a dark line above the face but does not frame it; the frame is at the neck.
  • Shirt: pinstripe, bengal, candy, gingham, tattersall; small scale. Roll necks, polos and knits: plain, or a Breton stripe on a crew neck or T-shirt.
  • Tie: repp stripe, pin dot, foulard, paisley, small houndstooth. Bow tie: pin dot, foulard, paisley, small houndstooth. Cravat (ascot), worn inside an open collar: paisley, foulard, pin dot. All small scale, and all should be darker than the shirt. Scarf: paisley, herringbone, houndstooth, up to medium scale.
  • Jacket and trousers: nailhead, birdseye, herringbone, pin and chalk stripe, houndstooth, glen check, windowpane; up to medium scale. Overshirt: also gingham. Coat: up to large scale.
  • Socks: birdseye, pin dot, Breton stripe, herringbone. Pocket square: pin dot, foulard, paisley, houndstooth.
  • Paisley is a figured pattern: the teardrop (boteh) with a curled tail. Foulard is a small repeated geometric figure, traditionally printed on silk. Both count as patterns like any other: scale, contrast and the two-pattern limit apply. A paisley in warm colours (gold, rust) can come to the face only inside the warm theme on a cool dark base (Style Guide, section 8a).

B Which pattern in which cloth. A pattern also has to exist in its cloth: Valet offers only those that do, and the rules flag one that does not (from an imported file, say). Weave patterns (nailhead, birdseye, herringbone, houndstooth, glen check) are the structure of woven wool, and a few are also found in tweed, linen or silk. Corduroy, moleskin and denim carry none, because their ribs or twill already are their surface. Shirting stripes and checks (bengal, candy, gingham, tattersall) belong to cotton and linen shirtings; seersucker is itself a puckered stripe or check; printed figures (pin dot, foulard, paisley) are silk or wool challis; and the regimental stripe is woven in silk repp. A pattern the chosen cloth cannot carry is shown greyed out, and changing to such a cloth makes the piece plain, with a note. Each pattern also keeps to the repeat it is made in (a pinstripe about 0.6 to 2 cm, a windowpane 3 to 14 cm), so the scale slider stops there.

B Near the face, at most two patterns, with at least one plain layer. Two patterns need not share a colour: the writers who state the rules of pattern mixing govern it by scale and kind (Permanent Style, 2009; Put This On, 2010, whose model outfit mixes tan, blue and red). What they do recommend is that colours relate and are picked up, subtly, somewhere in the outfit (Gentleman's Gazette; Permanent Style), without obvious matching (Boyer, True Style). So Valet judges whether a pattern's colours belong with the rest under colours together (section 10), and adds a hint that never lowers a verdict: a tick when a pattern's colour is picked up elsewhere, a suggestion when it is not. The usual menswear limit is three (Rampley & Co call it a ceiling rather than a target); two is the Style Guide's deliberate choice for a bald head. The pocket square counts among them: it sits a hand from the face.

A B Different scales. Two patterns of similar size interfere, near the face and also where a patterned jacket or knit meets patterned trousers at the hips (unless they are one suit): the eye cannot separate them, and close stripes can shimmer (moiré). Vision science explains why: the eye analyses patterns through channels tuned to size (Campbell and Robson 1968), and a pattern weakens the visibility of others of similar size, not of very different ones (Blakemore and Campbell 1969). The working rule that the larger repeat should be about twice the smaller is tailoring convention: that patterns worn together should differ in scale is stated by Styleforum Journal, Put This On and Rampley & Co; the factor of two is Valet's. Two patterns of different kinds (a stripe and a check) and in different colours stay apart even at one scale, as Put This On notes ("color makes up for that"), so for them the same scale is a note rather than a failure.

B Pattern contrast follows the face. A pattern near the face whose own light-dark contrast is above the upper bound of section 4 is louder than the face it frames.

On a video call

A B A camera records the world as a grid of pixels, and detail finer than two pixels per cycle cannot be recorded: it turns into false, moving stripes (aliasing, seen as moiré). A webcam framing your head and shoulders spreads about 84 cm over 1280 pixels at 720p, about 0.66 mm a pixel. Valet warns when a cloth has strong detail with a period under four pixels (2.6 mm). That is the sampling science; the four-pixel margin and the strength cut-off are working values. Fine stripes, small checks, herringbone and birdseye in strong contrast are the usual culprits, which matches the advice given to people appearing on television.

Do vertical stripes slim?

A Valet does not score stripe direction, because the evidence runs against the popular rule. In 1867 Helmholtz observed that a square filled with horizontal lines looks taller and narrower than the same square filled with vertical ones. Thompson and Mikellidou (2011) measured it with eight observers, each making about a thousand judgements: a square of vertical stripes had to be made about 7% taller to look as tall as one of horizontal stripes, and a square of horizontal stripes about 4.5% wider to look as wide as one of vertical stripes. On a line drawing of a woman, the horizontally striped figure had to be about 6% broader at the hips to look the same; on photographed mannequins, about 11%. On real striped cylinders the picture changed: horizontal stripes were judged close to their true width, and vertical stripes made the cylinders look wider, so there was no slimming in either direction.

A Later work mostly agrees. With photographs of a real woman, horizontal stripes and a darker dress each made her look slightly thinner, more so together and against a dark background (Koutsoumpis, Economou and van der Burg 2021). In about 1,000 web photographs of people of known weight, judged by crowd workers, horizontal stripes made no measurable difference to estimated weight, while dark clothes lowered it slightly (Martynov, Garimella and West 2020). Ashida, Kuraguchi and Miyoshi (2013) found the thinning from horizontal stripes stronger on slim figures than on heavy ones, where it may even reverse, weaker when heavy figures were shown first, and different from observer to observer. The honest summary: horizontal stripes do not widen you, vertical ones do not reliably slim you, and either effect is a few per cent, small next to contrast and scale. Most of these studies used women's figures; none tested a man in a striped jacket.

13. Proportion

Proportion works out, from your measurements, where each garment should end and how wide its parts should be. Clothes change how proportions read, not the body: the page describes your proportions in neutral words and never scores them.

Your proportions. Each measurement and ratio is placed among the 4,082 men of ANSUR II, the 2012 US Army anthropometric survey (Gordon et al. 2014), whose data are public: "near the middle", "above the middle" and so on. Its waist and waist height are taken at the navel, as Valet's are. Its shoulder breadth is taken straight between the shoulder bones, while Valet's follows the curve of the back, so shoulders are not placed. It is one population, and the comparison says only where you sit among those men: soldiers, younger and fitter on average than men in general, so a civilian older man may sit higher on waist and lower on chest-to-waist drop than among his peers. Civilian 3D-scan surveys such as SizeUSA and SizeGermany are larger and broader but not public, which is why Valet uses ANSUR II.

Missing measurements are estimated from your height with the survey's typical proportions (back-neck to floor about 86 % of height, inside leg about 48 %, navel waist about 60 %), and every target that uses an estimate says so. Ranges widen with how hard a measurement is to take at home.

Periods. Classic menswear is not one set of proportions. Each period cut its pieces to go together: the short sweaters, waistcoats and ties of the 1930s ended at a high trouser waistband worn with braces, and look short only against today's lower rise. So the targets are worked out from each other within a period: the waistband sets where the knit, the waistcoat and the tie end; the jacket's shoulder sets the lapel, and the lapel the tie. Six periods: today's classic, the 1930s drape, the 1940s, the 1950s Ivy sack suit, the 1960s slim cut and the 1970s. The historical figures are period tendencies drawn from tailoring histories (broad or natural shoulders, lapel widths, where the waistband sat, how full the leg was), given as ranges and graded B; check them against period garments before relying on a number.

  • B Jacket length: half the back-neck-to-floor height, covering at least three quarters of the seat; at the shorter end when the legs are shorter than most for the height, which leaves them more length.
  • B Waist button: at the natural waist or a little below; lower, it shortens the legs.
  • B Shoulder, lapel, tie: the shoulders frame the head; the lapel reaches about halfway from the collar to the shoulder line (wider in the 1930s); the tie matches the lapel (Flusser, Dressing the Man, chapter 3).
  • B Collar: it counterbalances the face: a broad face takes longer points and a moderate spread, a long face a wider spread; without hair, a taller band. A face is called broad or long only when it stays so after the tape's error (about 4 mm on width and length, enough to move a middle face to the 70th percentile); the same holds for the lens depth below.
  • B Glasses: the frame front about as wide as the face at the cheekbones, so the temples neither bow out nor leave a gap; a frame wider than the face makes it look narrow, a narrower one makes it look broad. The depth of each lens counterbalances the face, as the collar does: deeper for a face long for its width (36 to 42 mm), shallower for a broad one (30 to 35 mm), 32 to 38 mm in between. The millimetres are working values within the usual range of men's frames; varifocal lenses usually need about 30 mm or more, so the optician has the last word. The frame's weight is judged against your contrast in section 4, and the shape of the frame is not prescribed (section 16).
  • B Rise: the waistband is where the legs appear to start; at or a little above the body's waist it reads longer-legged. The evidence is indirect: studies of dresses, not trousers. In one, Japanese and British participants judged dresses with the waistline moved up or down on a three-dimensional avatar, and the Japanese preferred it higher than the body's own waist, the British at it or 1 cm above (Kim, Numakura, Lim and Takatera 2020, a conference paper: women's dresses moved by 1 to 2 cm, and two small groups that differed in more than nationality). Nothing comparable has been published for men's trousers that we found, so the rule stays B. The seat: with a prominent seat or a hollow lower back the back of the trousers has further to go than the front, so look for a higher back rise, side adjusters or braces; a flat seat wants a trimmer seat. Valet asks for the seat as you see it (flat, average, prominent) rather than for pelvic tilt, which is hard to judge on oneself and is not Valet's to diagnose.
  • B Trouser hem: the opening covers a good part of the shoe; turn-ups deeper on taller men; a narrower leg breaks sooner.
  • A What clothes do to the eye is smaller than the folklore. Horizontal stripes make a figure look slightly taller and narrower, not wider (section 11). Dark clothes make people look a little lighter in weight in real photographs (Martynov, Garimella and West 2020) but not taller: in the one study of men in suits, light against dark suits made no difference to estimated height, while a single-colour outfit did raise it (Lee 2018, a doctoral study with photographs of people in suits, in street scenes and on a plain background). Valet uses these for advice on continuity, not for promises.
  • B In the Dresser, a Proportion section. Lines: where the outfit changes from light to dark falls on the body, and the eye reads the figure in those pieces. A strong step at a jacket's hem over the hips starts the legs below the waist; a low waistband does the same; a strong step from trousers to shoes ends them early; with legs shorter than most for the height these are worth changing, otherwise they are only noted. A tonal column, every main piece within 15 L* of the others, is marked as the one change found to add height (A for the finding, B for the 15 L* that defines a column). Cuts: trousers have a rise (high, mid, low) and knits a length (short, regular, long), set in the piece's editor; a short 1930s knit over mid- or low-rise trousers leaves a band of shirt (a failure, with both fixes offered), a long knit hides a high waist, and a standard tie hangs past a high waistband. The line notes never mark a colour as a clash. Your shape: with a collared shirt, a face clearly broad or long for its width gets the collar that balances it, and a head with no hair the advice of a taller band; a full stomach is told that a mid or low waistband rolls under it, and high rise on braces sits above it. A prominent seat or a hollow lower back is told that low-rise trousers gap at the back of the waistband and pull under the seat, with a higher rise as the fix. Notes only: the outfit does not record the collar's cut.
  • B Also on the page: the gorge (higher for a shorter figure, mid or lower for a tall one); the jacket's balance for a forward or very erect posture (front and back lengths, a collar that sits close); the hat brim and the size of a jacket pattern by frame (tall or broad: the upper end; short or narrow: the lower end; the Dresser's pattern-size rule follows the same frame). Each target says which measurements it comes from, so you know which to take carefully. Try it under the figure moves a jacket hem and a waistband and says where they land.
  • Left out on purpose: head height and neck length (no measurement in the survey matches how they would be taken at home, and the collar rule works without them), comparing shoulders with the survey (measured differently), estimating lengths from photos, and a separate "dark below, light at the face" rule (the colour rules already favour it).
  • A The golden ratio is not used: experiments have tested it and found against it. From Fechner in the 1860s on, experiments on rectangles and proportions have found no reliable preference for it once the way of asking is controlled (reviewed by Green 1995). For faces, the most attractive spacing of the features turned out to be close to the average face, not the golden ratio (Pallett, Link and Lee 2010). Valet places your body among a survey of real men instead, and calls no position better.

14. Fit before you buy

The Fit tab checks whether a garment will fit you before you buy it: a jacket, overcoat, shirt, knit, trousers, shoes or a hat. Fit is judged garment by garment, so it has its own page rather than a place in the Dresser, which judges how pieces go together.

What it compares. The measurements a shop gives for a size, from its size chart: the garment's own measurements, not the body sizes it is said to fit. Up to four sizes from one chart can be entered side by side; the page names the best of them, with the mildest verdict and the smallest misses. Each measurement is judged in the most reliable way available:

  • A Against one of yours that fits. Measure a garment of the same kind that fits well, the same way, and fill in the first column. It carries your build and the ease you like, so it beats any rule: within 2 cm around, or 1.5 cm in length, it should fit as yours does. This is the method tailors and made-to-measure shops ask for.
  • B Against the Proportion targets for the lengths and widths Proportion works out: jacket length, shoulders and sleeve, overcoat and knit length, trouser rise and leg opening, for your build and the period chosen on the Proportion tab. A knit longer than the period's, which ends at its high waistband, is marked wearable rather than wrong: it is right over lower trousers.
  • B Against your body plus ease. Chest, seat and the like: the garment minus the body is its ease, and the ease decides the cut it will have on you (slim, modern or classic), since the same jacket is slim on a broad man and classic on a narrow one. An overcoat needs a jacket's ease plus room for the jacket; a knit less than a jacket.

B Which periods it suits. The main verdict uses the period chosen on the Proportion tab. Under each size, a second line runs the measurements that change with the period (lapel width, rise, leg opening, knit length, and a little the shoulders and jacket length) against all six periods, and names those whose range each falls within, give or take a centimetre, and those the whole garment suits. A 1930s-cut pair of trousers checked under today's classic then reads "rise too high", with "suits the 1930s and the 1940s" beneath it. Lapel width is optional, as few shops give it, and it is a matter of style: a lapel of another period never fails a size, because another size would not change it. The period ranges are tendencies (B), so the line means "in the range for", not "authentic to".

Alterations. Each miss says whether a tailor can mend it: sleeves and trouser hems easily, waists within the cloth folded inside, shoulders, collars, jacket length and knitwear hardly at all. The verdict follows: fits, fits with an easy alteration, close with alterations, or try another size.

Size labels. Without a chart, the label alone can be checked: European jacket sizes are half the chest they are cut for (A), UK and US sizes the chest in inches; collars and trouser waists name the body size; shoe sizes name the last, about 1.5 cm longer than the foot (European in Paris points of 2/3 cm, UK as 3 × the last in inches − 25, US men's one more); hats the head, in cm or in the US as inches over π in eighths. Lasts and cuts differ between makers, so a label is a guide and the measurements are the test.

Measuring flat. Charts often give a chest or waist laid flat, across one side; tick measured flat and it is doubled. A number far below your body is flagged as probably flat. Lengths run from the base of the collar at the back; charts that start at the shoulder's highest point give about 2 cm more.

15. Your sizes and the try-on

The Fit tab turns the measurements from the You tab into sizes, checks a garment before you buy it (section 14), and lists what to look for at a try-on. Everything is a starting point: brands cut differently, and a garment's own measurements beat any label. Numbers also miss what a fitting sees. Most bodies are a little asymmetric (one shoulder lower, one arm longer), posture varies beyond the three shape settings on the Proportion tab, and two cloths cut to the same measurements hang differently. Treat the sizes and checks as a compass for the fitting room, not a replacement for it.

  • A German and Italian jacket sizes are half the chest in centimetres (100 cm is size 50). German long sizes, for tall slim men, are numbered 2 × size − 2 (52 becomes 102); short sizes half the size (26).
  • B UK and US jacket sizes are the chest in inches; the letter (Short, Regular, Long) follows height. Some brands advise an inch smaller for a slimmer fit.
  • B Shirt collar: the neck plus about half an inch (1.25 cm), so one finger fits (T.M. Lewin; Charles Tyrwhitt's size tables).
  • B Drop: suits sold as a set pair the jacket with trousers a fixed amount smaller, given in inches even in Europe: drop 6 (about 15 cm) is what shops stock as standard, so it is read off the size labels rather than any writer; a slimmer cut is drop 7 or 8 (about 18 or 20 cm). When your chest-to-waist difference is larger, a standard suit gapes at the trouser waist.
  • B Ease and cut. A garment is larger than the body by its ease. Tailors add roughly 10 to 18 cm to the chest of a jacket depending on the cut (Nathan Tailors; Proper Cloth). Valet reads the cut a garment will have on you from its ease: for a jacket's chest, 6 to 10 cm is slim, 10 to 14 modern (tailored), 14 to 19 classic; less is tight, more hangs. Shirts and trouser seats have their own ranges. The same jacket is slim on a broad man and classic on a narrow one.
  • B What a tailor can and cannot do. Each finding says whether it can be altered: sleeves and trouser hems easily; waists within the cloth folded inside, often 2 to 4 cm; shoulders, collars and jacket length hardly or not at all. A wrong size in those is the wrong size.
  • B Lengths: a jacket sleeve ends just before the wrist bone with 1 to 1.5 cm of shirt cuff showing (Rampley & Co); a jacket is about half your back-neck-to-floor height long; trousers about 3 cm shorter than your inseam to the floor rest on the shoe in a slight break.
  • A Hats are sized by head circumference in centimetres; US sizes are that in inches divided by π, rounded to the nearest eighth (7⅛, 7¼…), and British sizes run an eighth smaller. A B Shoes: European sizes count the last in Paris points of two thirds of a centimetre (a definition, A); the last is usually about 1.5 cm longer than the foot (a maker's convention, B). Lasts differ, so shoes are always a try-on.

16. Popular rules and what the evidence says

Some rules are repeated so often that they feel proven. Where studies contradict one, or nobody has tested it, Valet says so here rather than dropping it silently. The badge on each says how far the rule itself has been tested: A where experiments have tested it, whatever they found (stripes, red, the golden ratio); C where it is a popular system nobody has tested, so that only indirect evidence bears on it (seasons, wrist veins, face-shape frames). Each entry gives the rule, what was found, and what Valet does.

"You are a Winter" (seasonal colour analysis) C
Found: the system goes back to Itten's colour teaching and was popularised around 1980 by books such as Carole Jackson's Color Me Beautiful. Its seasons are defined by description, not by measurable limits, and we found no study of whether two analysts put the same person in the same season, or whether the season's colours are judged better on that person than others. Absence of evidence, not proof against. The one direct experiment on clothing colour and skin (Perrett and Sprengelmeyer 2021) supports only the broad idea that fairer skin suits cooler colours. Valet: keeps the four dimensions continuous, because the boxes force people near a border into the wrong one, and does not score by season. The temperature rule (section 7) is the part closest to the tradition, so it is badged B, carries a confidence label and never decides a tier alone.
"Blue wrist veins mean cool, green mean warm" C
Found: veins carry dark red blood, yet look bluish through skin. Measurements and light-scattering simulations show that the colour they appear depends on how the skin scatters and absorbs light at each wavelength, on how much oxygen the blood carries, on the vessel's width and depth, and on how the eye judges colour in context (Kienle et al. 1996). Depth and lighting vary from person to person independently of the skin's pigments. That the test fails as a guide to undertone is our inference from this physics; no study has tested the vein test against measured skin colour that we found. Valet: mentions it only as a rough home test and measures the skin beside white paper instead (section 2).
"Vertical stripes slim, horizontal stripes widen" A
Found: close to the reverse. Horizontal stripes make a figure look slightly taller and narrower; vertical stripes do not reliably slim; the effects are a few per cent (section 11). Valet: does not score stripe direction.
"Black is slimming" A
Found: true, but small. In real photographs dark clothes lowered estimated weight slightly (Martynov, Garimella and West 2020); a darker dress made a model look a little thinner, more so against a dark background (Koutsoumpis et al. 2021). Valet: does not score it. A few per cent of apparent weight is small next to how a colour sits at your face, which the tiers judge.
"A dark (or a light) suit makes you look taller" A
Found: in the one study of suits, light against dark made no difference to estimated height; a single-colour outfit did raise it (Lee 2018). Valet: marks a tonal column as the one change found to add height (Proportion, section 13).
"Wear red to look attractive" A
Found: a meta-analysis of the "red romance" studies found a small effect of red on men's ratings of women and a very small one on women's ratings of men, with signs that published results overstate it and that it has shrunk in newer studies (Lehmann, Elliot and Calin-Jageman 2018). How much people like a colour as a patch, blues most and dark yellows and browns least, is a separate matter again (Palmer and Schloss 2010). Valet: gives red no bonus and keeps "liked" apart from "suits you": tiers come from your colouring only.
"Choose frames that contrast with your face shape: angular for a round face, round for a square one" C
Found: the rule comes from optician and retail advice. Its face shapes (oval, round, square, oblong, heart) are defined by description, with no measurable limits, the same weakness as the seasons, and we found no study that tests the matching table. The few experiments on frames ask other questions. One fitted 16 frame designs on one man's and one woman's face: the glasses made the faces look more symmetrical, but not more attractive (Lo et al. 2012). Another varied frame features on faces of three types and found that the frames shifted impressions of approachability, assertiveness and masculinity (Lo and Chu 2014). Frames do change how a face reads, then, but nothing supports a table of shapes. Valet: does not use face-shape categories for frames. It measures the face instead: frame width against face width, lens depth against the face's proportions (section 13), and the frame's weight against your contrast (section 4).
"Dark clothes near the face deepen dark circles under the eyes" C
Found: the claim comes from seasonal colour analysis. No study has measured whether the lightness of a collar changes how visible under-eye shadows look, and no established menswear writer states it that we found. Valet: does not judge it. Until version 1.8 the Dresser flagged an outfit dark everywhere near a face with a marked shadow under the eyes; that rule was removed. With deep-set eyes, the daylight test for the bottom rim of glasses on your profile remains, since it checks your own face.
"The golden ratio is the ideal proportion" A
Found: no reliable preference for it in more than a century of experiments (Green 1995); the most attractive face is close to the average one (Pallett, Link and Lee 2010). Valet: does not use it (section 13).

Where Valet departs from the tradition. The tradition judges contrast by eye and by type. Valet measures it, which forces choices the books never had to make: where the bands fall, how much the eyes count, what a half-grey head adds up to. Those choices are listed in section 3 as working values. If the daylight tests on the You tab disagree with a verdict, the daylight is right.

Welcome to Valet

Valet works out which colours suit you, from colour science and the classic menswear rules, and checks whole outfits against them: colour, contrast, pattern, cloth, formality and season. It runs in this browser and keeps nothing anywhere else.

What it takes. A minute to begin: your skin alone gives a first reading, and every feature can be chosen by name instead of photographed. The fifteen measurements are optional and only feed Proportion, Fit and the outfit's proportion notes; three of them (shoulder width, arm and back-neck to floor) need a second person. Or look around with the demo profile first.

How to use it

  1. You. Set your colouring. Take a photo in daylight with a sheet of white paper beside your face and sample paper, skin, hair (or brows) and eyes; or choose each by name. Add glasses, redness or deep-set eyes if they apply. Winter or summer, at the top, switches to your tanned skin.
  2. Palette. Every named colour by where it can go on you: T1 anywhere, T2 the one deliberate colour, T3 below the belt, T4 away from the face. Your core palette sits on top. Put colours in the study to see which go together, or build around one colour.
  3. Proportion. With your measurements (You tab), where each garment should end and how wide its parts should be, for your build and a period from the 1930s to today.
  4. Fit. Before you buy: a shop's measurements or size label against you, or against one of yours that fits. Your sizes, and what to check at a try-on.
  5. Dresser. Put an outfit together piece by piece, head to feet: what each piece is, its cloth, colour and pattern, and the accessories. The checks ask the same questions in order (temperature, contrast, frame, sheen, pattern, metal, then the feet); lock what you own and let Valet choose the rest. Save the outfits you like under Saved outfits, grouped by season and occasion, or print a one-page card.

The lightness chart beside each tab shows your colouring and the outfit on one scale of light to dark. Point at any colour to try it; every “?” explains its control. Save to a file on the You tab keeps your colouring, palette and outfit; How it works gives the theory and its sources.

What it cannot do. A colour reading from a photo is only as good as the light it was taken in. Numbers cannot see one shoulder lower than the other, feel how a cloth hangs, or know how a house cuts its sizes, so the sizes are ranges to take into a fitting room, not a replacement for one. And it covers everyday dress, from casual to evening; the codes of black tie, white tie and morning dress are not checked. How it works, section 1, says more.

Version 1.8. Proportion and Fit are still being refined: their numbers are tailoring rules and survey averages, so treat them as a starting point and trust a garment that fits you.

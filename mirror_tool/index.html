About Tessera · privacy

A workshop for ornament: make tiles with hinged mirrors from the plates of Combinaisons ornementales (Verneuil, Auriol & Mucha, 1901), cut mosaics in the Roman and trencadís manners, and compose both into surfaces.

Tessera has no accounts, no cookies, no analytics or advertising, and no tracking. It never sends your work anywhere and shares no data with anyone.

  • Files you save (projects, mosaics, PNG and SVG) go only where you save them.

    The workflow

    Tessera has three tabs that feed one another. Tile maker and Mosaic maker make tiles; Saved tiles (the library, in the Compose panel) keeps them; Compose lays them out on a board into a finished surface.

    1. Look around. On a first visit the Compose board holds a demo. Click a piece to select it; the Mosaic maker tab holds the demo’s mosaic panel. When you are ready, Clear the workshop (top of the Compose panel) and choose whether the demo’s tiles stay in the library.
    2. Make a tile from mirrors in the Tile maker tab. Add an ornament from the 1901 plates (or your own image) to the sheet, choose a method (Verneuil’s hinged mirrors, Brewster’s kaleidoscope, or a wallpaper group) and set the mirrors; the reflections appear on the right. Under As a tiled surface pick the tile shape (triangles, squares, hexagons or strips) and press Save tile. The tile joins Saved tiles. Sections 1 to 6 explain the geometry.
    3. Make a mosaic in the Mosaic maker tab: cut one from an image (Roman rows, Roman stones following the outlines, or trencadís), lay one by hand from baskets of random pieces (free form), or turn text into mosaic (lettering). Edit it piece by piece, then Add to Compose as a tile (it joins Saved tiles in the shape of its space) or Place in Compose as a panel (it goes straight onto the board, at its own size).
    4. Compose. Drag tiles from Saved tiles onto the board. Pieces snap corner to corner; cut, resize, turn, flip and duplicate them; Fit to an edge… sizes a group of pieces to cover another piece’s edge exactly. Every tile can go back to where it was made: ✎ on a saved tile, or Edit in the … tab with its pieces selected, opens a mosaic in the Mosaic maker and a mirror tile in the Tile maker, with its mirrors, method and ornaments as they were. Save back then updates the tile and every piece that uses it. This works for tiles from a project file too, as the set-up travels with the tile (tiles saved before version 1.0 have no recorded set-up).
    5. Keep and export. Your work stays in this browser between visits: the library, the board and the open mosaic. Save project writes everything (with the tile images) to one file that Open project reads back on any computer; Add tiles from file takes only the tiles from such a file. The surface exports as PNG or SVG, the mosaic as PNG, SVG or its own file.
    6. Tidy the library. Under Show, list one kind of tile (a method, the mosaics, or the demo tiles); the ✕ on a tile removes it, Remove the tiles shown removes everything listed. Pieces on the board that used them go too; Undo brings those back, and Load the demo always restores the demo.

    1 · What the mirrors show

    Two flat mirrors stand upright on the sheet, hinged at a point O and opened to an angle α. Only the wedge of the sheet between them is real; everything else you see is reflections of that wedge. A reflection of a reflection is a rotation, so the images alternate around O: wedge, mirrored wedge, wedge turned by 2α, mirrored again, and so on, in both directions. The right-hand panel draws exactly this “unfolding”: each slice is the sheet rotated or reflected into place.

    2 · Seamless angles: α = 180°/n

    α = 45° = 180°/4: 8 slices, the two chains meet behind the hinge and agree. Symmetry D4.
    α = 72° = 360°/5: 5 slices, but behind the hinge two plain (unmirrored) copies touch: the motif breaks along the red seam.

    The two chains of images, going round from either mirror, meet again behind the hinge. They agree only if a whole number of pairs (slice + mirrored slice) fills the circle, i.e. if 2α divides 360°, i.e. α = 180°/n. Then you see 2n slices and the result has the symmetry of a regular n-gon with its mirror lines, the dihedral group Dn. Brewster stated it for his kaleidoscope (patented 1817; Treatise, 1819): the angle must be an “even aliquot part” of the circle. For any other angle the chains disagree and the tool draws a red seam.

    3 · Verneuil's dial (360°/k)

    The preface's dial marks the mirror openings 360°/k for k = 3 … 8 (120°, 90°, 72°, 60°, 51.4°, 45°), promising a triangle, square, pentagon, hexagon… For even k these are seamless angles (360°/k = 180°/(k/2)). For odd k (120°, 72°, 51.4°) they leave a seam behind the hinge, unless the motif in the wedge is itself symmetric about the line bisecting the mirrors, as the lines B A B in Verneuil's own figure are. So the dial is right for his example and wrong as a general rule. Try the red chips under “Verneuil's dial marks” with any asymmetric ornament.

    4 · Borders and corners

    Two parallel mirrors repeat the strip between them forever in both directions: an endless border (mirror lines across the band only). A single mirror placed across a border at 45° turns it through a right angle: this is the preface's corner at CC; at any other angle the corner is not square.

    5 · What mirrors alone can make

    Mirrors generate only symmetries that are reflections and their compositions, so the possibilities are short and completely known:

    • Two hinged mirrors: rosettes Dn (α = 180°/n).
    • Two parallel mirrors: one type of border; adding a mirror along the band gives one more.
    • Three or four mirrors closing a cell fill the whole plane, but only four cells work, because every corner must be 180°/n: the equilateral triangle (60-60-60), the triangles 90-45-45 and 90-60-30, and the rectangle. These give the four wallpaper patterns built purely from mirrors (p3m1, p4m, p6m, pmm). A Brewster kaleidoscope with three mirrors at 60° is the first one.

    Everything else in ornament (rotations without mirrors, glide reflections, the remaining 13 wallpaper types) cannot come from mirrors.

    Symmetric ornaments. Each ornament in the library carries a badge for its own symmetry, in the usual notation for figures with a centre: Dn has n mirror lines and n-fold turns (D1 = one mirror line, a left–right figure; D3 = three lines, a trefoil), Cn has n-fold turns and no mirror (a swirl); no badge means none. The badge is measured, not taken from the book: the picture is reduced to ink, centred on its centre of mass, and compared with its own copies turned by 360°/n and reflected in every line through the centre; the share of ink that lands on ink, corrected for what chance overlap gives at unrelated angles, has to pass 38% (a “≈” marks results under 50%, symmetric only roughly, as hand drawing is). The labels were then checked by eye and some forty corrected, in both directions. C∞ marks a ring of like leaves, which turns into itself by any angle, roughly. Of the 274 ornaments, about 100 have at least one mirror line, a few more have turns only, and most have no symmetry at all: Verneuil drew for the mirror, which supplies the symmetry.

    Why it matters for the wallpaper groups: at a rotation centre of order n the group lays n turned copies of whatever is there on top of each other, and on a mirror line a figure and its reflection. An ornament placed exactly on such a point stays whole only if its own symmetry already contains that operation, so a C4 or D4 ornament on a ■, a D3 on a meeting of three mirrors, a D1 with its axis along a mirror line. Anywhere else, away from all centres and mirrors, any ornament works and the group spreads its copies apart. Under the group menu the tool lists which sites the chosen group has and how many library ornaments fit each; the library filter “fits …” shows them. Each symmetric ornament also carries its centre and the direction of one of its mirror lines, found by searching for the best overlap; the selected ornament shows them on the sheet (a ringed dot, dashed lines). With “Snap symmetric ornaments” on (Brewster and wallpaper modes), an ornament dragged near a site it fits is pulled onto it and turned so its mirror lines lie along the group's; one added from the library goes straight to the most symmetric site it fits. Hold Alt (Option) to drag freely. Gaudí's panot is laid out the same way: its three figures are separate ornaments, each three-fold, each on its own class of third-turn centre.

    Your own images. The picker's “My images” tab takes your own drawings, scans or photos (several at once, or dropped onto it). They are reduced to at most 1600 px, given a symmetry badge in the same way, and kept only in this browser's storage on this device: nothing is uploaded. “Save library” writes all of them to one .json file; “Import library” adds such a file back, in another browser or on another computer (images already there are skipped). Clearing the browser's site data deletes them, so keep a saved copy.

    6 · From rosette to tiles

    The tile in the lower panel is the mirror view cut to a regular triangle, square or hexagon centred on the hinge. All tiles are identical (squares and hexagons are shifted, every other triangle is turned 180°, as when laying real tiles). Two neighbouring tiles continue each other across their joint exactly when the rosette has a mirror line through its centre perpendicular to that joint, because then the neighbour is the reflection of the tile in the joint. With α = 180°/n the rosette has n mirror lines, so:

    60°3breaksseamlessseamless

    Squares need n even, triangles and hexagons need n divisible by 3. Five-fold rosettes (36°) never tile seamlessly: no regular tiling has five-fold symmetry. “Seamless” means the lines meet; a curve crossing a joint at a slant still makes a corner there (it meets its own reflection), so the smoothest tiles come from motifs whose lines cross the mirror lines at right angles. The tile size slider moves the cut, which changes where the joints fall in the design.

    Strips (narrow rectangles) suit the parallel pair. The border it makes repeats every two mirror spacings, so a strip running from one mirror line to the next-but-one, centred on the mirror line between, is one full repeat: strips laid end to end continue each other. Turned 180°, such a strip is the same as its reflection in its long edge, so rows stacked with every other row turned also meet seamlessly. Unlike rosette tiles, this works for any motif and any spacing. Strips can also be cut from a rosette when n is even.

    With stacked rows the decorated surface has mirror lines across the strips every spacing d and along every row joint: it is a rectangular mirror pattern, as if made by four mirrors standing in a rectangle (wallpaper type pmm).

    Brick bond (each row shifted by half a strip) is different. The border repeats only every 2d, and over a half-shifted span it shows the two halves of the repeat in the opposite order, which neither the strip nor the strip turned 180° can supply. So the row joints break, unless the motif between the mirrors is itself symmetric (then the border repeats every d) or nothing in the design reaches the long edges. The tool checks the edges and tells you. For strips cut from a rosette, brick bond breaks as well.

    Tiles from Brewster’s cells. A closed cell of mirrors makes a pattern that repeats on a lattice, so the tile offered is one lattice cell: a square, a rectangle, or (on the hexagonal lattice) the hexagon of points nearer one lattice point than any other. Identical cells are only shifted by lattice vectors, and every lattice shift is a symmetry of the pattern, so they meet without a seam whatever the drawing. Other shapes need more: a tiling by triangles, or by strips stacked with every other row turned, places half the tiles turned 180°, and that turn must itself be a symmetry of the pattern (a half-turn centre on each joint). Three mirrors at 60° (p3m1) have no half-turns at all, and on the other cells the centres must sit exactly on the joints; a tile laid anywhere else breaks. The cell is the one shape that needs nothing but the lattice.

    The question of how identical decorated tiles combine has its own history: Sébastien Truchet's Mémoire sur les combinaisons (1704) and Dominique Douat's Méthode pour faire une infinité de desseins différents (1722) counted the patterns made by one two-coloured square tile in different orientations; brick bonds themselves are classified by their wallpaper groups (Coxeter, 1969).

    7 · Composing, the demo and mosaics

    “Save tile” (under the tiled surface in the Tile maker tab, or + Tile from the Tile maker in Compose) keeps the current tile, cut exactly as shown, for the Compose tab. There you drag tiles onto a board and combine shapes: snapping brings a corner of the piece you move onto a nearby corner and lines up the edges. Pieces of equal edge length then fit edge to edge: squares beside triangles, hexagons ringed by squares and triangles, or a band of squares, then hexagons cut in half through their corners (whose long edge covers two squares) alternating with whole hexagons standing on a flat side, then whole hexagons. Each piece can be cut (half, diagonal), resized, turned and flipped. With “Resize to fit edges” on, a piece whose corner snaps onto a neighbour’s corner, with an edge running along the neighbour’s edge, is resized so that edge is exactly as long as the neighbour’s, or exactly ½, ⅓ … ⅛ of it (or 2× … 8×), so that n pieces fill the edge: narrow strips line up under a row of half-hexagons without adjusting the size by hand. When the automatic fit cannot guess what you mean, for example a group of strips that should together cover one hexagon edge, use Fit to an edge…: select the pieces (Shift-click or Shift-drag), press the button, then click the edge of another piece. The selection is turned to lie parallel to that edge, scaled and moved so that its side facing the edge covers it exactly from end to end. Shift-click several edges lying in one line (say the bottoms of two strips) to fit a piece across all of them. The pattern is scaled, never stretched; Undo reverses a fit. Any tile opens again for changes where it was made (✎ on the tile, or Edit in the Tile maker tab / Edit in the Mosaic maker tab for selected pieces), and Save back updates it everywhere; pieces on the board keep their size. Duplicate a piece, move the copy into place, and Duplicate again repeats the same step, which builds rows quickly. Projects are saved as a single JSON file containing the tile images, so they reopen exactly; the finished surface exports as PNG, or as SVG with the joints as vector lines.

    The demo and the workshop. On a first visit the board holds a demo: a mosaic panel, cut following the outlines, with two rosettes and a butterfly from the plates inside a double border and the words Ver Sacrum laid with the Lettering tool, framed by mirror tiles (squares, whole and half hexagons) and trencadís hexagons that close into a rectangle. Clear the workshop (top of the Compose panel) empties the board and the Mosaic maker tab and asks about the library: keep every tile, remove only the demo’s tiles, or empty it. Undo brings back the board and the removed tiles, and Load the demo puts the demo back at any time. To tidy the library itself, choose under Show what to list (one method, mosaics, or the demo tiles) and press Remove the tiles shown.

    Mosaics have their own tab, Mosaic maker. From an image: choose a picture (From the library… opens the ornament library, the whole plates with their crop tool, and My images; From a file… takes any image) and a style. Roman, in rows (opus tessellatum) sets roughly square stones in rows. Roman, following the outlines (opus vermiculatum) lays the stones in rows that run along the edges of the shapes: the image is reduced to its palette colours, the borders between them become guide lines, and rows are laid at one, two, three… half-stones from the nearest border, turned to follow it, before smaller cut pieces fill what is left. Trencadís, the broken-tile mosaic of Gaudí and Jujol, uses irregular shards. Each piece takes the palette colour (k-means in CIELAB) nearest to the average of the image under it.

    Free form starts from an empty space, a rectangle, square, hexagon or triangle of a given size. Extend to this size changes the space without scaling the pieces: it grows (or shrinks) around them. Three baskets (small, medium, large) hold random pieces: shards, tesserae, pebbles or a mix. Click one, then click in the space to lay it in the chosen colour; the basket hands you another of that size. Hold the mouse button and drag to paint with pieces: they are laid one after another along the stroke, a piece’s width apart, skipping places where they would overlap, and Undo takes the whole stroke back (Shift-click keeps the same shape, R turns it). Toss refills a basket with new random pieces until you like them; Toss shapes (T) does the same for pieces already laid, keeping their size and colour. The piece in hand can be turned with ⟲ ⟳ or the slider (or R, Shift+R); its angle stays for the next pieces, or Random turn gives each new one its own. A guide image can lie faintly under the space, as a cartoon lies under a real mosaic: lay the pieces on it, set how strongly it shows, and, if you like, let each piece take the guide’s colour under it. The guide stays with the open mosaic in this browser but is never part of the mosaic or its exports.

    An image can also be laid into a mosaic that is already open: choose it under From an image, set Width when added, press Add to the current mosaic and click where it should go. It is cut with pieces of the mosaic’s own size, only where the image is not transparent; its colours are matched to the mosaic’s palette (new colours are added), and the ground around it is re-laid.

    Lettering turns text into mosaic. Type the text (several lines if you like), choose the lettering (Roman capitals, Art Nouveau, serif, bold sans), the letter height and the piece size, and a style: following the letters runs the rows of stones along the strokes, as in Roman inscriptions; rows and trencadís are also offered. Make a text mosaic starts a new mosaic of the text, with or without background pieces; Add to the current mosaic lays the letters wherever you click in the open mosaic, lifting out the pieces underneath (Undo puts them back).

    All mosaics follow the same rules, however they were made, so the techniques mix freely: a mosaic cut from an image can take lettering, another image and pieces from the baskets, and a hand-laid one can take an image or text. In every mosaic pieces may touch but never overlap: a new piece goes to the nearest free spot (or is refused when there is none), dragged pieces stop at their neighbours, turned and tossed pieces must fit, and text or an image laid in lifts the pieces it covers and re-lays the ground only where it fits. To make room in a full mosaic, delete some pieces first. The mosaic is then edited piece by piece: select pieces (click, Shift-click, Shift-drag a box; double-click a colour to select all its pieces) and click a colour to give it to them, or use Paint to click and drag colour over them (Alt-click picks a colour up). Pieces can be moved, turned, deleted and added from the baskets, also in a mosaic cut from an image; the palette colours, the mortar and the joint width can be changed for the whole mosaic. Add to Compose as a tile saves it with your tiles in the shape of its space, to be combined with the other tiles; Place in Compose as a panel puts it on the Compose board at its own size, as a rectangle. A mosaic tile selected in Compose offers Edit in the Mosaic maker tab; there, Save back updates the tile and every piece that uses it. The mosaic in the tab is kept between visits; Save mosaic and Open a mosaic file exchange it as a file (a project file from Compose opens too), and PNG and SVG export it, the SVG with every piece as a vector shape.

    8 · Methods and sources: what is what

    Every pattern in the tool is labelled with the method that made it, and the label travels with saved tiles, pieces on the board, project files and exports, so a design can stay within one historical practice or mix them knowingly.

    LabelWhat it isSource
    Verneuil 1901Two mirrors hinged at an angle, two parallel mirrors, or one mirror, moved over a drawing. Rosettes Dn, borders, corners.Verneuil, Auriol & Mucha, Combinaisons ornementales (c. 1901), preface.
    Brewster 1819Three or four mirrors closing a cell (“polycentral kaleidoscopes”): square, rectangle, 60°-60°-60°, 90°-45°-45°, 90°-60°-30°. They fill the plane; in modern terms the wallpaper groups pmm, p3m1, p4m and p6m, the only ones mirrors alone can make.     Wallpaper groupAll 17 plane symmetry groups, including rotations without mirrors and glide reflections. A mathematical classification used here to generate patterns, not a period instrument: no arrangement of plane mirrors produces p1, p2, pm, pg, cm, pmg, pgg, cmm, p4, p4g, p3, p31m or p6. Glide lines are drawn dashed. Gaudí’s hexagonal panot (1904), which repeats by third-turns only, is group p3.E. S. Fedorov (1891); G. Pólya (1924); operations as in the International Tables for Crystallography, Vol. A; orbifold symbols after J. H. Conway.

    Verneuil’s open pair of mirrors moved over a drawing and Brewster’s closed cells of three or four mirrors are physical instruments described by their authors; the wallpaper groups are a classification, used here generatively. Patterns made by turning tiles rather than reflecting them belong to the groups mirrors cannot reach, for example the quarter-turn tiles of Lewis F. Day’s “turn-round” (Pattern Design, 1903, ch. XII), group p4, and Gaudí’s hexagonal panot, group p3. Tiles saved with a method no longer offered are marked “method not recorded”.
